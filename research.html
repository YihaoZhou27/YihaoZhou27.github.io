Research Highlights

Brief introductions to selected research projects.

The ASTRID Simulation at $z=0$

ASTRID is one of the largest hydrodynamical cosmological simulations evolved to $z=0$. It hosts $2\times5500^3$ particles in a box with 370 Mpc per side. It hosts a large population of massive black holes (MBHs), spanning from $10^4$ to $10^{11}$ M$_\odot$, and includes a black hole dynamical friction model that properly captures MBH orbital evolution. The large dynamic range of Astrid on one hand allows detailed studies of galaxy and massive black hole evolution at high resolution, and on the other hand allows systematic studies of rare massive systems with the large cosmic volume. Astrid is now used to provide predictions or interpretations of current and future observations, such as the Pulsar Timing Array, Laser Interferometer Space Antenna (LISA), and the Rubin Observatory's Legacy Survey of Space and Time (LSST), James Webb Space Telescope (JWST). I am very grateful to have the opportunity to run the last chunk of ASTRID down to $z=0$. The simulation datasets are now publicly available at https://astrid.psc.edu.

MAGICS: Massive Black Hole Assembly in Galaxies Informed by Cosmological Simulations

MAGICS simulation suite bridges the gap between cosmological simulations and small-scale MBH dynamics. We select 15 MBH seed merger events from ASTRID that are detectable by upcoming LISA, and reproduce them using high-resolution idealized simulations. Besides the higher resolution, we also adopt improved gravity solvers, including the regularized integrator KETJU and the $N$-body code TAICHI. This allows us to trace the MBH dynamics down to sub-parsec scales in realistic galaxy environments of LISA sources. Based on MAGICS, we found that the MBH dynamics is significantly affected by the surrounding stellar environment, and that nuclear star clusters (NSCs) can significantly accelerate MBH seed mergers. This highlights the importance of understanding the MBH dynamics in realistic galaxy environments, and the need for high-resolution simulations to study this.

AMBRA: ASTRID with MBH Seeding from BRAHMA

AMBRA combines the large cosmological volume of ASTRID with gas-based black hole seeding models from BRAHMA. Motivated by JWST discoveries of massive BHs at $z\gtrsim 9$, AMBRA adopts a lenient heavy-seed prescription that allows $4\times 10^{4-5}$ M$_\odot$ seeds to form in star-forming, metal-poor gas. Compared with ASTRID, this model forms seeds earlier and more efficiently, and produces some of the massive black holes detected by JWST at high redshifts ($z\gtrsim 8$). AMBRA simulation produces a population of Little Red Dots (LRDs); which allows us to have a systematic study of the evolution of these objects.

PTA Continuous Wave Sources

We use the MBH population in ASTRID to predict continuous wave (CW) sources for PTA experiments. Now that PTAs have reported evidence for a low-frequency gravitational wave background, individual CW sources are the next anticipated signal. We evolve the MBH binaries in ASTRID, model their GW emission, and compute the detection probability (DP) for PTA observations. The highest-detectability CW sources are hosted by central galaxies in massive galaxy clusters: they come from mergers of $M_{\rm BH}>10^{10}$ M$_\odot$ black holes emitting in the lowest frequency bins ($f<10$ nHz), and their hosts are galaxies with stellar mass above $10^{12}$ M$_\odot$ sitting at cluster centers. One particularly striking case in ASTRID is a triple merger, where two consecutive mergers happen within 500 Myr in the same cluster core and produce high-DP signals at both $\sim2$ nHz and $\sim10$ nHz. We also look at the electromagnetic counterparts of these events, which appear as single or dual AGN in massive, star-forming host galaxies, pointing to concrete targets for future multi-messenger searches for PTA CW sources.

Predicting LISA Detection Rates

We use ASTRID to forecast the MBH mergers that LISA will detect down to $z=0$. Because ASTRID directly models MBH dynamical friction and seeds black holes down to $5\times10^4$ M$_\odot$, it gives realistic merger trajectories and a fairly complete census of the LISA population. We find that these binaries enter the LISA band with high eccentricities, peaking around $e_0=0.8$ at all redshifts. Accounting for eccentricity raises the event rate from 5.6 yr$^{-1}$ (assuming circular orbits) to 10.5 yr$^{-1}$, mostly through inspiral sources that only coalesce after the LISA mission, which make up 46% of the detections and highlight how much LISA's sensitivity to the early inspiral matters. Most events come from $10^{5-6}$ M$_\odot$ black holes at low redshift ($z<2$) with low mass ratios ($q\sim0.01$–$0.1$). Eccentricity also extends LISA sensitivity toward higher masses, up to approximately $10^9$ M$_\odot$, and shifts the peak of detectable mergers to $z_{\rm peak}=0.8$, implying that the most massive LISA events may also be PTA sources. Their electromagnetic counterparts are mostly AGN with $L_{\rm bol}>10^{42}$ erg s$^{-1}$, though only 1% exceed $10^{44}$ erg s$^{-1}$; the brightest ones are exactly those rare LISA/PTA events with $M_{\rm BH}>10^8$ M$_\odot$.
